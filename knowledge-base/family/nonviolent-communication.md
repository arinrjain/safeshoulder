# Nonviolent Communication (NVC): A Guide to Resolving Conflict

## What is Nonviolent Communication?

Nonviolent Communication (NVC) is a framework for expressing yourself honestly while also hearing others with compassion. It transforms conflicts by focusing on **needs** rather than blame or demands.

**Core belief:** Everyone's needs matter equally. When we communicate from needs, people listen differently.

## The Four Components of NVC

### 1. Observation (What happened?)

State the **specific behavior** you observed, without judgment or interpretation.

**Not this:**
- "You never listen to me"
- "You're so selfish"
- "You always ignore my feelings"

**Try this:**
- "When you interrupted me three times during our conversation..."
- "When you didn't respond to my text for two days..."
- "When I saw you looking at your phone while I was talking..."

**Key:** Be specific and factual. Avoid words like "always," "never," "selfish," "rude."

### 2. Feelings (How did it affect you?)

Share your **emotional response** to the behavior.

**Authentic feelings:**
- Frustrated, hurt, scared, disappointed, lonely, anxious, angry, sad, confused

**Not actual feelings (they're judgments):**
- "You make me feel like I'm stupid" (that's a judgment)
- "I feel ignored" (that's usually a thought, not a feeling)

**Try this:**
- "When you interrupted me, I felt frustrated because..."
- "I felt hurt and sad when you didn't respond"
- "I felt anxious and worried when you looked away"

### 3. Needs (What matters to you?)

Identify the **underlying need** that wasn't met.

**Common needs:**
- Understanding, respect, connection, autonomy, safety, honesty, trust, support, appreciation, to be heard, fairness, growth, peace

**How it sounds:**
- "I need to feel understood and respected in our conversations"
- "I need consistency and reliability in our relationship"
- "I need to feel safe expressing my opinions"

**Why this matters:** Conflicts rarely are about the surface issue. They're about unmet needs. When you name the need, the other person can hear it differently.

### 4. Request (What do you need now?)

Make a **clear, specific, doable request**—not a demand.

**Not this:**
- "Be a better listener" (vague)
- "Care about me more" (not actionable)
- "Stop being so selfish" (accusatory)

**Try this:**
- "Would you be willing to put your phone away when we talk?"
- "Could you let me finish my thought before responding?"
- "Would it help if we set a specific time daily to check in?"

**Key:** Make it specific enough that someone could say yes or no clearly.

## Putting It Together

**Formula:**
"When [observation], I felt [feeling] because I value/need [need]. Would you be willing to [specific request]?"

**Example 1:**
- Observation: "When you didn't tell me about plans until the last minute"
- Feeling: "I felt anxious and excluded"
- Need: "I need to feel included and have time to prepare"
- Request: "Could you involve me when making plans and give me a few days' notice?"

**Example 2:**
- Observation: "When you didn't acknowledge my promotion at work"
- Feeling: "I felt disappointed and sad"
- Need: "I need to feel celebrated and have my achievements recognized"
- Request: "Would you be willing to celebrate my wins with me?"

## Receiving Communication with Compassion

When someone shares something difficult with you:

### 1. **Listen without defending**
- Don't interrupt to explain your side
- Don't make it about you
- Focus on understanding, not agreeing

### 2. **Reflect back what you heard**
- "So you felt hurt when I cancelled? You value spending time together?"
- "It sounds like reliability is important to you"

### 3. **Ask clarifying questions**
- "What do you need from me?"
- "What would feel better?"
- "Is there something specific I could do?"

### 4. **Take responsibility for your part**
- You don't have to agree with everything
- But you can acknowledge their experience was real to them
- "I understand you felt hurt. I didn't intend to hurt you, but I did, and I'm sorry."

## Common Scenarios

### Family Conflict
**Instead of:** "You're so controlling!"

**Try:** "When you criticized my job choice, I felt hurt and defensive because I value your respect. Would you be willing to support my decisions even if you'd choose differently?"

### Workplace Issues
**Instead of:** "You never give me credit!"

**Try:** "When you presented my ideas without mentioning my contribution, I felt undervalued because I value recognition for my work. Could you include my name when sharing ideas I developed?"

### Romantic Relationships
**Instead of:** "You don't care about me!"

**Try:** "When we go a week without meaningful conversation, I feel disconnected because I need to feel emotionally close to you. Could we set aside time each week to really talk?"

### Friendships
**Instead of:** "You're selfish!"

**Try:** "When you've cancelled on me three times, I felt hurt because I value our friendship and time together. Are things okay? I need to feel like I matter to you."

## Tips for Successful NVC

1. **Assume good intent** - Most people aren't trying to hurt you
2. **Slow down** - Rushing escalates conflict
3. **Stay curious** - "Help me understand why this matters to you"
4. **Focus on needs, not positions** - "I need autonomy" vs "You should let me do what I want"
5. **Own your feelings** - "I feel hurt" not "You hurt me" (they didn't cause it; they triggered it)
6. **Be specific** - Vague complaints trigger defensiveness
7. **Ask permission** - "Can I share how I experienced that?" shows respect
8. **Practice gratitude** - Name what you appreciate about them too

---

## Difficult Conversations: A Structure

1. **Ask permission first:** "Can we talk about something that's been on my mind?"
2. **Make the request:** Use the four-step NVC formula
3. **Listen to their experience:** Don't defend or explain
4. **Find common ground:** "It sounds like we both value respect/connection/honesty"
5. **Problem-solve together:** "What would work for both of us?"
6. **Commit to action:** "So I'll do X, and you'll do Y. Can we check in next week?"

---

## When NVC Doesn't Work

NVC assumes the other person is willing to engage. If someone:
- Becomes abusive or threatening
- Refuses to engage
- Dismisses your feelings repeatedly

You may need to:
- Set boundaries
- Involve a mediator
- Seek professional help
- Prioritize your safety

**Remember:** You can do NVC perfectly and still have someone choose not to engage. That's their choice. You can't control it, but you can control using honest, compassionate communication.

---

*Nonviolent Communication was developed by Marshall B. Rosenberg. It's based on the understanding that all people have legitimate needs, and conflict comes from different strategies for meeting those needs.*
