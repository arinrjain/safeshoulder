# CBT Thought Records: A Guide to Cognitive Restructuring

## What is a Thought Record?

A thought record is a tool used in Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) to help you identify and challenge unhelpful thinking patterns. It works by breaking down situations into thoughts, feelings, and behaviors to find where you can make changes.

## How Thoughts Affect Feelings

Your thoughts about a situation directly influence how you feel and behave:

1. **Situation** → What happened? Be specific and factual.
2. **Automatic Thought** → What thought popped into your head first?
3. **Feeling** → What emotion did you experience? Rate intensity 0-100.
4. **Behavior** → What did you do in response?

## The Thought Record Worksheet

### Step 1: Describe the Situation
- **When did it happen?** Date and time
- **What happened?** Stick to facts, not interpretations
- **Who was involved?**

Example: "Monday at 3pm, my manager didn't comment on my report during the meeting"

### Step 2: Identify Automatic Thoughts
These are the immediate thoughts that spring to mind—often without evidence.

- What went through your mind?
- What does this mean about me?
- What's the worst that could happen?

Example: "My manager hated it. I'm going to get fired. I'm bad at my job."

### Step 3: Notice Your Feelings
- **Emotion:** (e.g., anxious, sad, angry, ashamed)
- **Intensity:** Rate 0-100% (0 = not at all, 100 = maximum)

Example: Anxiety 85%, Shame 70%

### Step 4: Gather Evidence

**Evidence For** (supporting the thought):
- What facts support this thought?
- What happened previously that seems to confirm it?

**Evidence Against** (challenging the thought):
- What doesn't support this thought?
- What would a friend say?
- Have I handled difficult situations before?

Example:
- For: "I worried about feedback before"
- Against: "My last 3 reviews were positive. My manager asked me to lead a new project."

### Step 5: Develop a Balanced Thought

Using the evidence, create a more realistic, balanced perspective:

Example: "My manager was quiet, but that doesn't mean it was bad. I've gotten positive feedback before. I should ask for specific feedback rather than assuming the worst."

### Step 6: Re-rate Your Feelings

After developing a balanced thought, how do you feel now?

- **Emotion:** (same emotion)
- **New Intensity:** Rate 0-100%

Example: Anxiety 40%, Shame 20%

## Common Thinking Traps to Watch For

1. **Catastrophizing** - Assuming the worst will happen
   - Challenge: "What's the evidence? What's most likely?"

2. **Mind Reading** - Assuming you know what others think
   - Challenge: "Can I read minds? What actual evidence do I have?"

3. **All-or-Nothing Thinking** - Seeing things as all bad or all good
   - Challenge: "Is there a middle ground?"

4. **Overgeneralization** - One event means everything is terrible
   - Challenge: "Is this always true? Or just this time?"

5. **Should Statements** - "I should," "I must"
   - Challenge: "Says who? What if I didn't?"

## Balanced Thoughts - Key Phrases

- "I don't know for certain that..."
- "It's possible that..."
- "Even if that were true, I could..."
- "The most likely explanation is..."
- "I've handled difficult situations before..."
- "I can manage this by..."

## Practice Tips

1. **Start small** - Use this on situations that bother you but aren't your biggest worry
2. **Be honest** - Write what you actually think, not what you should think
3. **Collect evidence actively** - Notice moments that support both the anxious and balanced thoughts
4. **Repeat** - The more you practice, the more natural it becomes
5. **Use a journal** - Track your automatic thoughts and see patterns over time

## When to Seek Help

If you notice:
- Thinking patterns are persistent despite practice
- Feelings are getting worse
- You're isolating yourself
- Thoughts are about harming yourself

Please reach out to a mental health professional.
